# Acts: The Gospel on the Move

> Compiled study notes from previous Bible-study conversations. This is a reconstructed thematic summary, **not a verbatim export** of all chat transcripts. Check each passage against Scripture before teaching or publishing.

## Overview

**Author:** Luke (traditional attribution). **Narrative movement:** witness empowered by the Holy Spirit, from Jerusalem toward Rome (Acts 1:8). **Study-series title:** *Acts: The Gospel on the Move*.

**Missionary journeys (approximate narrative divisions):** first journey, Acts 13–14; second, Acts 15:36–18:22; third, Acts 18:23–21:16; journey and ministry in Rome, Acts 27–28.

## Acts 2 — Pentecost and the early church

**Passages:** Acts 2:1–4, 2:5–13, 2:14–41, 2:42–47.

- Pentecost corresponds to the Jewish Feast of Weeks; compare Exodus 23:16 and Leviticus 23:15–21.
- The Spirit's coming fulfills the promise of God; Peter cites Joel 2:28–32.
- Jews and proselytes from many regions hear the mighty works of God in their own languages (Acts 2:5–11).
- Peter proclaims Jesus' death, resurrection, and exaltation, calls for repentance and baptism, and approximately 3,000 respond (Acts 2:22–41).
- The believers devote themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers; they generously share resources (Acts 2:42–47).

**Study question:** How does Pentecost advance the mission described in Acts 1:8?

## Acts 3 — Healing and repentance

**Passages:** Acts 3:1–26.

- Peter heals a man lame from birth in the name of Jesus (3:1–10).
- Peter speaks in Solomon's Portico, part of the temple complex (3:11).
- The healing directs attention to the risen Jesus rather than to apostolic power (3:12–16).
- Peter calls for repentance and turning to God (3:19), connecting Jesus with the prophets and God's covenant promises (3:18, 22–26).

## Acts 4 — Spirit-empowered boldness

**Passage:** Acts 4:31.

- Believers who already know the Spirit are freshly empowered to speak God's word boldly.
- The Acts narratives do not present tongues as an explicit accompanying sign every time someone is filled with the Spirit; compare Acts 2:4, 4:8, 4:31, 10:44–46, 13:9, 13:52, and 19:6.
- The church's generosity and shared provision are also highlighted (4:32–35).

## Acts 8 — The Ethiopian official

**Passage:** Acts 8:26–40.

- Philip explains Isaiah to the Ethiopian official and announces Jesus.
- The official's precise Jewish/Gentile religious status is debated; avoid claiming certainty beyond the text.
- Acts 8 anticipates the expanding mission, while Acts 10 provides an especially public confirmation of Gentile inclusion.

## Acts 10 — Cornelius and Gentile inclusion

**Passages:** Acts 10:1–48.

- Cornelius is a devout Gentile centurion; Peter receives a vision challenging purity boundaries (10:1–16).
- Peter learns not to call people unclean whom God has accepted (10:28, 34–35).
- Peter announces forgiveness through Jesus to everyone who believes (10:43).
- The Holy Spirit falls on Gentile listeners, who are then baptized (10:44–48).
- Compare Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 for the food-law background; the immediate narrative application is the acceptance of Gentiles.

## Acts 11 — Peter's defense and Antioch

**Passages:** Acts 11:1–30.

- Peter explains the Spirit's reception by Gentiles (11:15–18).
- The gospel spreads to Antioch; Barnabas encourages believers and Saul joins the teaching ministry (11:19–26).
- Disciples are first called Christians at Antioch (11:26).
- The church provides practical relief during a predicted famine (11:27–30).

## Acts 14 — Opposition and perseverance

**Passages:** Acts 14:1–28.

- In Iconium, some believe while opponents stir up hostility (14:1–7).
- At Lystra, after a healing, the crowd identifies Barnabas with Zeus and Paul with Hermes; the apostles reject worship and direct attention to the living Creator (14:8–18).
- Paul is stoned and left for dead but continues the mission (14:19–20).
- The missionaries strengthen disciples, teach perseverance through hardship, and appoint elders in the churches (14:21–23).
- **Clarification from prior discussion:** Herod is not a figure in Acts 14; the Herod narrative is in Acts 12.

## Acts 16 — Guidance, conversion, and deliverance

**Passages:** Acts 16:1–40.

- Timothy joins the mission (16:1–5).
- The Spirit redirects travel; Paul's Macedonian vision leads the team westward (16:6–10).
- Lydia, a seller of purple goods from Thyatira, responds in Philippi and is baptized with her household (16:11–15). Believers later gather at her home (16:40); the text does not establish that all later Philippian gatherings were there.
- Paul casts a spirit out of an enslaved girl; the loss of profit provokes opposition and imprisonment (16:16–24).
- Paul and Silas pray and sing in prison. After an earthquake, the jailer asks how to be saved and hears the call to believe in the Lord Jesus (16:25–34).
- Paul invokes his Roman citizenship when the authorities attempt a quiet release, and the magistrates come to apologize (16:35–40).

**Questions:** Why did Paul wait to assert his citizenship? How did God use imprisonment to bring the gospel to the jailer's household?

## Acts 17 — Thessalonica, Berea, and Athens

**Passages:** Acts 17:1–34.

- Paul reasons from Scripture about the Messiah; responses vary (17:1–9).
- Bereans examine the Scriptures to evaluate Paul's message (17:10–12).
- In Athens, Paul engages an idolatrous city and speaks at the Areopagus, referring to an altar inscribed 'To an unknown god' (17:16–23).
- Epicurean and Stoic philosophers hear him (17:18). Paul presents God as Creator and Sustainer, calls all people to repentance, and points to the resurrection and coming judgment (17:24–31).

**Question:** How does Paul communicate biblical truth to an audience without the same scriptural background as a synagogue audience?

## Acts 18 — Corinth, coworkers, and Apollos

**Passages:** Acts 18:1–28.

- Paul meets Aquila and Priscilla in Corinth; they share the trade of tentmaking (18:1–3). Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome explains their recent move.
- Paul teaches in the synagogue; opposition leads to a sharper focus on Gentiles, while Crispus believes (18:4–8).
- The Lord encourages Paul not to fear and promises his presence; Paul remains about eighteen months (18:9–11).
- Gallio, proconsul of Achaia, dismisses an accusation against Paul as a dispute over Jewish religious law (18:12–17). Gallio's known tenure helps place this episode around AD 51–52.
- Paul departs with Aquila and Priscilla, visits Ephesus, and continues traveling (18:18–23). Paul's haircut is associated with a vow (18:18), though the text does not fully specify the vow's details.
- Apollos is eloquent and knowledgeable but needs more accurate instruction; Priscilla and Aquila explain God's way to him more fully (18:24–28).

**Themes:** perseverance, God's presence, ministry partnerships, humble teachability.

## Acts 19 — Ephesus, the Spirit, and spiritual authority

**Passages:** Acts 19:1–41.

- Paul meets disciples who know John's baptism but are not yet familiar with the Holy Spirit as described in the passage (19:1–7). The precise status of these disciples before Paul's instruction is debated.
- They are baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus; when Paul lays hands on them, the Holy Spirit comes on them and they speak in tongues and prophesy (19:5–6).
- **Earlier study distinction:** water baptism in Jesus' name (19:5) should be distinguished from the Spirit's coming (19:6); compare Acts 2:38, 8:12, 10:47–48. Compare Romans 8:9 and Ephesians 1:13–14 for the Spirit's relation to belonging to Christ.
- Paul teaches in the synagogue and then in the lecture hall of Tyrannus after resistance (19:8–10).
- The sons of Sceva try to use Jesus' name as a formula without genuine authority; the evil spirit responds that it knows Jesus and recognizes Paul (19:13–16). The narrative warns against treating the name of Jesus as a magical technique.
- Public confession and the burning of magic books follow (19:17–20).
- The gospel's impact on local idol-related commerce leads to a riot (19:23–41).

**Questions:** What is the relationship between John's baptism and Christian baptism? Why does the spirit recognize Paul? How does Acts 19 relate to asking in Jesus' name (John 14:14)?

## Acts 20 — Farewell to Ephesian elders

**Passages:** Acts 20:17–38.

- At Miletus, Paul calls the Ephesian elders and reviews his humble, tearful, persistent ministry, both publicly and house to house (20:17–21).
- He expects suffering but wants to finish the ministry received from Jesus (20:22–24); compare 2 Timothy 4:7.
- Paul says he did not shrink from declaring the whole counsel of God (20:25–27).
- Elders are to guard themselves and the flock; the Holy Spirit has made them overseers to shepherd the church (20:28).
- He warns about fierce wolves and teachers who will distort truth (20:29–31).
- Paul commends them to God and the word of his grace, and models generosity rather than greed (20:32–35).
- The elders weep and accompany him to the ship (20:36–38).

**Historical observation:** Ephesus is Paul's longest clearly stated continuous ministry in Acts, approximately three years (20:31; compare 19:8–10). Corinth is described as eighteen months (18:11); Rome as at least two years (28:30).

## Acts 21 — Journey toward Jerusalem

**Passages:** Acts 21:4–14.

- At Tyre, disciples warn Paul about going to Jerusalem (21:4).
- In Caesarea, the prophet Agabus symbolically predicts Paul's binding and handover (21:10–11).
- Paul's companions urge him not to go, but he declares his willingness to suffer or die for Jesus' name (21:12–13).
- When Paul will not be persuaded, the group says, 'The will of the Lord be done' (21:14).
- **Interpretive question:** How should the Spirit's warnings about suffering be distinguished from the companions' conclusion that Paul should not proceed? Read Acts 20:22–24 and 21:4–14 together.

## Cross-chapter themes

1. **The Holy Spirit and mission:** Acts 1:8; 2:1–4; 4:31; 10:44–48; 16:6–10; 19:1–7.
2. **The gospel crosses boundaries:** Acts 8; 10–11; 14; 17.
3. **Persecution does not halt the gospel:** Acts 14:19–23; 16:19–34; 20:22–24; 21:10–14.
4. **Leadership and discipleship:** Acts 14:21–23; 18:24–28; 20:17–35.
5. **Scriptural reasoning and contextual communication:** Acts 3:12–26; 17:1–34; 18:4–6.
6. **Faith, baptism, and the Spirit:** Acts 2:38; 8:12; 10:44–48; 19:1–7. Interpret each narrative in its immediate context.

## Acts 4 — Spirit filling and speaking in tongues

**User question / discussion:** What does the filling of the Holy Spirit in Acts 4:31 mean, and must it always be accompanied by tongues?

**User-stated conclusion (recorded in an earlier study):** Every believer receives the Holy Spirit at conversion; Acts 4:31 depicts believers being filled or empowered for bold witness, and tongues are not a gift given to every believer.

**Textual basis / cross-references:** Acts 4:31; Romans 8:9; 1 Corinthians 12:29–30; Ephesians 1:13–14. Distinguish receiving the Spirit from later episodes of empowerment in Acts.

## Acts 5 — Opposition and obedience

**User questions / discussion:** What is happening in Acts 5:12–42? Who is the angel who releases the apostles (5:19)? Which Greek words carry the passage's emphasis?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** The apostles insist on obeying God rather than human authorities (5:29); they rejoice that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for Jesus (5:41); the preaching continues. The angel is best understood as an angelic messenger, though the text does not identify the angel by name. Greek study discussed *peitharchein* ('obey'), *archēgos* ('leader/pioneer'), and *kataxiōthentes* ('counted worthy').

**Open question:** Which of these themes did the user choose as the final sermon emphasis?

## Acts 8 — The Ethiopian official and what Scripture actually says

**User question:** Did the Ethiopian official go on to evangelize Ethiopia or found the Ethiopian church? Can we claim that from Acts?

**User-stated conclusion / explicit constraint:** **Do not teach that the Ethiopian official founded the Ethiopian church as an established biblical fact.** Acts says that he believed, was baptized, and went on his way rejoicing (8:35–39); it does not describe his subsequent ministry. Historical influence is possible, but Scripture is silent on the claim.

**Related study question:** How does Philip's encounter show Spirit-led evangelism (8:26–40)?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** God directs Philip; Philip obeys, listens, begins with the official's question about Isaiah, and proclaims Jesus from Scripture. Avoid presenting inferred later events as recorded events.

## Acts 14 — Idolatry, persecution, and church leadership

**User questions / discussion:** Why did the people of Lystra identify Barnabas as Zeus and Paul as Hermes? What happened when Paul was stoned? What does it mean that opponents 'poisoned' or embittered people's minds? Why did Paul and Barnabas appoint elders?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** The crowd interpreted a healing through its pagan religious framework (14:8–18); Paul and Barnabas refused worship. Opposition could turn public sentiment against the missionaries (14:2, 19). Paul continued preaching after being stoned (14:19–21). The missionaries strengthened disciples and appointed elders, showing that the mission included building durable churches (14:21–23).

**Open question:** No definitive personal conclusion about the crowd's change of attitude is recoverable.

## Acts 16 — Philippi and Roman citizenship

**User questions / discussion:** Why did Paul disclose his Roman citizenship after his imprisonment? Why did the magistrates apologize? What role did Lydia and her household play in the developing Philippian church?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** Paul and Silas were beaten and jailed, then the magistrates learned they were Roman citizens (16:35–39). Citizenship carried legal protections; Paul's insistence on a public response may have helped establish that the believers were not simply criminals, but the text does not state his full motive. Lydia opened her home (16:14–15, 40), making it an important gathering place for believers.

**Open question:** Paul's precise reason for delaying disclosure of citizenship is an inference, not a stated fact.

## Acts 17 — Athens and communicating the gospel

**User questions / discussion:** Who were the Stoics and Epicureans? What did the 'unknown god' altar mean? How did Paul communicate with an audience that did not share a Jewish scriptural background?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** Paul used an Athenian point of contact (17:22–23) without endorsing idolatry, then proclaimed the Creator, repentance, judgment, and resurrection (17:24–31). Epicureans and Stoics differed philosophically, but both heard Paul's proclamation.

## Acts 18 — Coworkers, Gallio, and the vow

**User questions / discussion:** Why were Aquila and Priscilla in Corinth? Where did they travel with Paul? Who was Gallio, and what was a proconsul? Why did Paul cut his hair in verse 18?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** Claudius's order drove Aquila and Priscilla from Rome to Corinth (18:2); they later traveled with Paul to Ephesus (18:18–19). Gallio was the Roman proconsul of Achaia (18:12). Paul's haircut is linked to a vow (18:18), but the text does not give all its terms; claims about a specific Nazirite procedure require caution.

## Acts 19 — Baptism, discipleship, and Jesus' name

**User questions / discussion:** Were the men Paul encountered disciples of John or Christian believers? Were they saved before meeting Paul? What does being 'baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus' mean (19:5)? How does this relate to John 14:14? How did the evil spirit know Paul (19:15)?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** Acts 19:1–7 distinguishes John's baptism from baptism in Jesus' name and narrates the Spirit coming upon these men. Their prior spiritual status is debated and should not be overstated. Acts 19:13–20 contrasts authentic relationship to Christ and divine authority with treating Jesus' name as a verbal formula. John 14:14 is not permission to manipulate spiritual power.

**Open question:** The user's final view on whether these men were saved beforehand is not explicitly recoverable; preserve the debate rather than invent agreement.

## Acts 20 — Elders and Paul's longest ministry

**User questions:** What is Paul's message to the Ephesian elders in Acts 20:17–38? Was Ephesus the place where Paul stayed the longest?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** Paul modeled humility, endurance, teaching, vigilance, and generosity. Elders must guard the flock from destructive teaching (20:28–31). Ephesus is the longest clearly documented continuous ministry stay in Acts (about three years, 20:31), though Acts does not provide a complete itinerary of Paul's lifetime.

## Acts 21 — Prophetic warnings and Paul's resolve

**User question:** How should Acts 21:4–14 be understood when disciples warn Paul not to go to Jerusalem and Agabus predicts imprisonment?

**Study takeaways (not verified as user-stated conclusions):** The passage distinguishes the prediction of suffering from the responses of those who love Paul. Paul is willing to suffer for Jesus (21:13), and the group finally entrusts the matter to God's will (21:14). Interpret the warnings alongside Acts 20:22–24; avoid asserting more about divine prohibition than the narrative establishes.
